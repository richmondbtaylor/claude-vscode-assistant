## [MISTAKE 1 — 1:00 to 4:00]

### Mistake #1: Automating the wrong process first.

I call this "automating the glamour process." Someone on the leadership team sees a demo — maybe it's an AI that handles customer emails, or a tool that generates reports automatically — and they go: *that's the one. Let's build that.*

The problem is that the most impressive-looking automation is almost never the one with the fastest ROI. The highest-ROI automations are usually unglamorous. They're data entry tasks. They're manual copy-paste workflows between two tools that don't talk to each other. They're the 45-minute-per-day task that six people on your team are each doing separately.

What you want to look for in your first 90 days is what I call a **high-frequency, low-variance process.** High frequency means it happens a lot — daily, or multiple times a week. Low variance means it follows a predictable pattern with few exceptions.

Customer email response is actually a terrible first automation because email is high-variance. Every message is different. The edge cases are endless. You'll spend more time managing exceptions than you save.

Compare that to something like: every time a lead fills out a form, route it to the right sales rep, log it in the CRM, and send a confirmation email. That's high-frequency, low-variance. You can automate it in a day and it works 98% of the time without touching it again.

**The fix:** Before you automate anything, map your team's most repetitive manual touchpoints. Count the hours. Pick the boring one. The boring one pays.

## [MISTAKE 2 — 4:00 to 7:00]

### Mistake #2: No baseline measurement before launch.

Here's a question: how many hours per week did your team spend on that process before you automated it? If you can't answer that with a real number, you have a problem.

This is the mistake that makes it impossible to defend your investment at the 90-day mark. You launch the automation, things feel smoother, the team seems less stressed — but when someone asks "what did we actually save?" you're stuck saying "it feels faster." That answer doesn't survive a budget review.

I see this constantly. Teams get excited about the tool and skip the measurement step entirely. They treat it like an IT upgrade — you just flip it on and assume it's better. But AI automation isn't a utility. It's an investment, and investments need baselines.

Before you automate anything, you need three numbers:

1. **Time cost** — How many hours per week does this process currently take, across all people involved?
2. **Error rate** — How often does this process produce mistakes, rework, or exceptions?
3. **Throughput** — How many units does the process handle? Leads, invoices, tickets, whatever the unit is.

You don't need anything fancy. A spreadsheet. A two-week time log. Even a rough estimate that everyone agrees on. The point is to have a number you can compare against after automation.

**The fix:** Set your baseline before you touch anything. Document it. Then at day 30, 60, and 90, measure against it. That data is what turns "this feels better" into "we recovered the cost in six weeks."

## [MISTAKE 3 — 7:00 to 10:30]

### Mistake #3: Treating AI outputs as final outputs.

This one is subtle, and it's become more common as AI tools have gotten more capable. The mistake is removing human review from the workflow entirely — or routing AI outputs directly to customers, systems, or stakeholders without a checkpoint.

Here's what happens. You set up an AI that drafts responses to customer inquiries. It works great in testing. The outputs look polished. So you set it to auto-send. And then three weeks in, a customer gets a response that's technically coherent but completely wrong for their situation — wrong product referenced, wrong pricing, wrong tone. Maybe it's a high-value client. Maybe it escalates. Now you're doing damage control, and the whole automation is under scrutiny.

The issue isn't that the AI made a mistake. AI tools make mistakes — that's expected. The issue is that there was no checkpoint to catch it before it caused harm.

In the first 90 days especially, every AI-generated output that touches something external — a customer, a financial system, a legal document, a public channel — should have a human review step. Not forever. But during the period where you're calibrating the tool, understanding its failure modes, and building trust with the system.

I think of it as a confidence curve. Day one, your confidence in the AI's outputs is low. Day 90, if you've been reviewing and the error rate is near zero, your confidence is high. That's when you can responsibly reduce the review layer.

**The fix:** Design your automation with a staged review process. Start with human approval on every output. After 30 days, review a sample. After 60 days, if the error rate is acceptable, consider moving to exception-only review. Earn the autonomy, don't assume it.

## [MISTAKE 4 — 10:30 to 14:00]

### Mistake #4: Underestimating the change management cost.

This is the one nobody wants to talk about, because it's not about the technology. It's about people.

You can build a perfect automation. Clean logic, reliable outputs, saves hours every week. And it will still fail if the people who are supposed to use it don't trust it, don't understand it, or feel like it's a threat to their job.

I've seen this play out in two ways. The first is passive non-adoption. The team technically has access to the tool, but they keep doing things the old way because the old way is familiar and the new way requires learning something. Nobody pushes back openly. The automation just... doesn't get used.

The second is active resistance. Someone on the team becomes the unofficial critic of the automation. They document every mistake it makes. They share those mistakes with peers. Over time, the narrative shifts from "this tool is mostly right" to "this tool can't be trusted." And because people remember errors more vividly than smooth operations, the narrative sticks.

Both of these are change management failures, not technology failures.

The fix here has two parts. First, involve the people who will use the automation in the design phase. Not just the decision-makers — the actual operators. They'll spot edge cases you'd miss, and more importantly, they'll have ownership over the outcome instead of feeling like it was imposed on them.

Second, be transparent about what the automation does and doesn't do. Don't oversell it. If you tell your team "this AI will handle all of X," and then it handles 80% of X with occasional errors, they'll feel misled. If you tell them "this AI will handle the routine 80% of X so you can focus on the complex cases," the same result feels like a win.

**The fix:** Treat adoption as part of the implementation. Budget time for training, feedback loops, and honest communication about limitations. Your ROI depends on whether people actually use the system — not just whether the system works.

## [MISTAKE 5 — 14:00 to 17:30]

### Mistake #5: Building in complexity too early.

The final mistake is the one that kills the most ambitious automation projects. It's the temptation to build the full vision before you've validated the foundation.

Here's how it usually goes. You've got a use case. Maybe it's automating your client onboarding. And as you're designing it, you start connecting pieces — intake form flows into CRM, CRM triggers a welcome email sequence, the sequence branches based on service tier, the service tier data feeds into a project management tool, the project management tool assigns tasks and sends Slack notifications, and on and on. You've built a beautiful, 14-step Rube Goldberg machine.

And then one piece breaks. The CRM field name changes. An API credential expires. The AI generates an output that doesn't match the expected format for the next step. And because everything is connected, one failure cascades through the entire workflow. You have no idea where it broke. Debugging takes days. The team loses confidence. The initiative stalls.

Complexity is where automations go to die. Especially in the first 90 days, when you're still learning what the system does in production — not just in testing.

The principle I follow is: **automate in slices, not systems.** A slice is one specific step in a process — form submission to CRM entry. Or CRM entry to email notification. One input, one output, one connection. Get that slice working reliably. Then, and only then, add the next slice.

This approach also gives you something valuable: proof points. Each working slice is a small, demonstrable win. You can show it to stakeholders. You can point to the time it saves. You build momentum and credibility in a way that one giant automation that maybe works can never do.

**The fix:** Scope your first automation ruthlessly. If it has more than five steps, break it in half and automate the more valuable half first. Complexity is earned through reliability, not built in from the start.

## [RECAP — 17:30 to 18:30]

Let's bring it back. The five mistakes:

1. **Automating the wrong process** — pick high-frequency, low-variance work first, not the flashy stuff.
2. **No baseline measurement** — you can't prove ROI without a before number.
3. **Removing human review too early** — earn autonomy through a staged confidence curve.
4. **Underestimating change management** — adoption is part of implementation.
5. **Building in complexity too early** — automate in slices, not systems.

None of these require more budget. None of them require a bigger team. They're discipline and sequencing. And the companies that get AI automation right in year one are almost always the ones that got these fundamentals right in the first 90 days.
